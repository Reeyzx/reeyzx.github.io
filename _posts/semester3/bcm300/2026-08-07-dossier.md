# TOLR - Final Project Dossier

---

## 1. Process of Ideation

*Tower of Literal Randomness* emerged from identifying a fundamental design flaw in the Roblox "Tower Obby" genre: a critical lack of replayability. Traditional tower obstacle courses present static, memorizable sequences where players either complete predetermined levels and receive "god powers," or restart from zero to replay identical courses. This design inevitably leads to player churn.

Our ideation process began with a core question: how can we maintain the familiar tower climbing fantasy while eliminating predictability? The solution was **procedural generation**, by replacing static level design with dynamic brick spawning and despawning mechanics. This ensures no two climbs are ever identical, transforming the experience from a memorization test into an adaptability challenge.

The core concept evolved through **three distinct prototyping milestones** prior to formal player testing. Initially, we built a minimal viable product (MVP) consisting of a tower on a generic platform, featuring three basic brick types **(Red = harmful, White = neutral, Blue = climbable)**. While mechanically functional, **internal developer trials** revealed this lacked player retention hooks. The second milestone introduced the "infinite run" meta-loop: reaching the 1000-stud summit allows players to press a return button to immediately initiate another climb from the ground, enabling continuous climbing within timed rounds. The third milestone addressed environmental context, replacing the void with a structured urban setting that synthesizes architectural theory with gameplay needs. **These internal prototyping phases established the foundational systems that were subsequently subjected to formal, external playtesting (detailed in Section 6).**

## 2. Theme, Setting & World-Building

*Note: Detailed world-building documentation is available in* ***[Blog Post 2](https://reeyzx.github.io/bcm300/2026/07/16/blog-tolr.html)****. This section provides a concise overview.*

### Theme
Following the framework established in Chapter 3, the theme represents the "what" of the game: an experimental extreme sports spectacle where "Red Bull-style" athletes conquer a fundamentally chaotic structure (Kwong, Chapter 3). This theme captures the lighthearted, viral energy of "GoPro 4K parkour" content, framing procedural randomness not as frustration but as the core appeal.

### Setting
The setting (the "where") is a Manhattan-inspired grid city that provides physical context for the spectacle. The 150x150x1000-stud tower occupies a central position within this urban environment, creating a vertical anomaly within a structured horizontal plane.

### World-Building Synthesis
Our world-building process applied Chapter 4's principle of "using real-world inspiration" while maintaining "consistency is key" (Kwong, Chapter 4). We adopted Barcelona's *Eixample* grid logic, designed by Ildefons Cerdà with strict 113.3x113.3m standardized blocks (Busquets, 2014). This rational geometric order creates a deliberate contrast with the tower's procedural chaos, framing the structure as an intentional anomaly within an ordered city.

However, traditional *Eixample* architecture created genre discrepancy with our blocky, Roblox-native aesthetic. To achieve visual and conceptual synthesis as advised in Chapter 6 (Kwong, Chapter 6), we pivoted to **Structuralism**. This mid-20th century movement emphasized "labyrinthine clarity" and basic, modular geometric forms (Frampton, 2007), aligning perfectly with our brick-based mechanics.

To address player retention and prevent claustrophobia-induced churn, we integrated a Central Park-style "Third Space" around the tower's base. Drawing on Frederick Law Olmsted's design philosophy of creating psychological refuges and "lungs of the city" (Rybczynski, 1999), this tranquil environment provides players a decompression zone between high-stakes climbing sessions.

## 3. Core Mechanics & Game Loop

### Mechanical Pillars
As categorized in Chapter 5, we structured the design into three distinct mechanical pillars: **core mechanics** (navigating spawning bricks), **progression mechanics** (XP and shop system), and **social mechanics** (multiplayer voting and leaderboard) (Kwong, Chapter 5).

### Core Mechanics
The fundamental interaction relies on four brick types:
- **Red (Harmful):** Causes player fall/reset
- **White (Neutral):** Standard platform
- **Blue (Climbable):** Primary progression surface  
- **Black (Trampoline):** Enhanced upward velocity

These bricks physically spawn and despawn after set durations within the 150x150x1000-stud vertical arena. This procedural generation eliminates memorization strategies, forcing players to exercise real-time spatial problem-solving and adaptability.

### The Infinite Run Core Loop

The game's loop structure evolved through iterative playtesting (see Figure 2). The MVP (Loop #1) featured only the core climbing mechanic. Following Playtest #1 feedback about a lack of retention hooks, we added the Round System (Loop #2) with voting and leaderboards. The long-term progression loop (Loop #3) integrates the shop economy.

This nested structure ensures players always have immediate goals (climb the tower), medium-term objectives (win the round), and long-term motivation (earn XP for shop items). The procedural brick generation within Loop #1 guarantees no two climbs are identical, addressing the replayability crisis identified in our ideation phase.

This synthesis of rules, mechanics, and narrative ensures all elements work together seamlessly, as emphasized in Chapter 6 (Kwong, Chapter 6). The infinite loop transforms what could be a frustrating single-attempt experience into an engaging endurance competition.

## 4. Three-Act Structure of Play

While Section 3 outlined the structural mechanics of the loop, this section analyzes the temporal pacing and emotional arc of the player experience specifically within Loop #2 (The Round System). Because a single climb from 0 to 1000 studs is a consistent, repetitive test of mechanical execution, the true dramatic pacing occurs at the macro-level of the 5-minute Round, not the individual ascent. This structure aligns with Chapter 6's workshop focus on prototyping the three-act game experience (Kwong, Chapter 6).

**Act 1: The Warm-Up (Setup)**  
The intermission and voting phase establishes rhythm. Players learn the specific spawn timings of the chosen gamemode with low tension, focusing on mechanical calibration. The first few runs serve as skill activation, allowing players to internalize brick patterns and test purchased skillsets.

**Act 2: The Grind (Conflict)**  
The mid-round phase tests endurance. Players constantly reset, competing to beat each other's altitude totals. Procedural randomness peaks as mental and physical fatigue set in. This act validates adaptability against the "literal randomness" of the tower, creating the core challenge that defines the game's identity.

**Act 3: The Final Broadcast (Resolution)**  
The final seconds create high-stakes tension. With leaderboard cutoffs on the line, players make frantic, high-risk pushes to secure XP payouts before the timer expires. The act culminates in the immediate dopamine hit of the results screen, seamlessly transitioning back to the intermission for the next competitive cycle.

## 5. Prototyping & Digital Materiality

### Grey-Boxing as Strategic Materiality
The physical prototype was developed entirely within Roblox Studio using **digital grey-boxing**. According to Chapter 7, digital materials fundamentally shape how players perceive and interact with a game (Kwong, Chapter 7). Our prototyping trajectory evolved through three phases:

**Phase 1: Temporary Grey-Boxing**  
Initial development used basic parts to validate spatial layout of surrounding scenery. The focus was purely to test whether the hypothetical cityscape could co-exist with the playing field without performance degradation.

**Phase 2: Research-Informed Grey-Boxing**  
Following our architectural research into Eixample and Structuralism, we discovered the environment's fundamental cuboid geometry allowed simple grey blocks to naturally emulate intended architectural massing. The strict grid layout meant placeholder buildings appeared intentional rather than unfinished.

**Phase 3: Validated Grey-Boxing**  
During Playtest 2, we observed a critical player behavior: climbers allocate cognitive focus entirely to procedural brick generation and jump timing, paying almost zero attention to peripheral scenery. This validated Chapter 7's principle that designers must prioritize functionality and player focus over excessive visual detail (Kwong, Chapter 7). The environment merely needed to provide spatial grounding—answering "whether something is there"—rather than demanding attention with high-resolution textures.

This was definitively confirmed during Playtest 3, where our public advertising successfully utilized screenshots of pure grey-box environments to attract over 4,300 players, proving visual sufficiency for the target audience.

### UI Iteration & Responsive Abstraction
A parallel iteration occurred within the User Interface materiality. For Playtest 1, the intro screen was designed as a complex, modernist art piece. While aesthetically striking, it proved highly time-consuming to produce and difficult to scale across devices.

Embracing Chapter 9's challenge of balancing creativity with technical constraints (Kwong, Chapter 9), we iterated by abstracting the layout into a strict 3x3 responsive grid. The four corner panels remain fixed, while the central five panels (forming a cross shape) flexibly scale according to screen resolution. This application of Chapter 8's abstraction principles simplified production complexity while ensuring consistent, high-quality design across all mobile and PC devices (Kwong, Chapter 8).

## 6. Playtesting & Iteration

### Playtest 1: Peer Testing & Cognitive Load (Pre-July 16)
**Context:** Closed testing with ~12 peers in the Minimal Viable Product (MVP) state (baseplate and tower only).  
**Observation:** While players were engaged, they explicitly asked if there were any "quirks" or alternative ways to scale the tower beyond standard WASD and Spacebar inputs. They expected a skill system, comparing baseline movement to other platformers.  
**Iteration:** We introduced a "Dash" skill button to test the cognitive load of adding UI skill prompts versus relying on baseline movement (Kwong, Chapter 8). This successfully shifted gameplay from passive reaction to active, strategic traversal, aligning closer to the "extreme sports" theme.

### Playtest 2: Developer Testing & Technical Constraints (July 19-21)
**Context:** Closed testing with ~8 players (developers and community members) possessing technical literacy.  
**Friction:** To elevate visual feel, we integrated an open-source camera module for stylized movement. However, the module's code directly interfered with Roblox's native engine camera-work, completely breaking "Shift Lock" (standard mouse-driven camera control). We spent three days (July 19-21) attempting fixes before isolating the engine interference through AI debugging.  
**Lesson:** This perfectly illustrates Chapter 9's challenge of balancing creative vision with strict technical constraints (Kwong, Chapter 9). We subsequently adopted a "no open-source package" policy for core systems.  
**Iteration (Progression):** To address retention, we implemented a foundational economy where XP is awarded in a lump sum upon round conclusion. With basic perks (e.g., Dash) priced at 200 XP, this ensures unlocking a skill requires sustained engagement over multiple runs, establishing fair, time-gated progression without removing player agency (Kwong, Chapter 5).

### Playtest 3: Public Alpha & Onboarding Funnel (July 25-26)
**Context:** Open alpha testing with ~4,300 unaffiliated players acquired via public advertising.  
**Metrics:** Advertising achieved a 3.18% Click-Through Rate (optimal by industry standards), with 58% conversion from click to active play. However, approval rating dropped from 96% (internal testing) to 75%, aligning with the baseline average for the "Obby" genre.  
**Observation (The "Leak"):** Analytics revealed severe onboarding failure. Players intuitively approached the tower and began climbing, but lacked instruction on colored brick meanings. Repeated, unexplained failures caused premature churn. Furthermore, while the shop was fully functional, players never looked beyond the tower to discover it.  
**Future Iteration:** To resolve this accessibility gap (Kwong, Chapter 4), we are developing a dedicated "Tutorial Place," an isolated environment guiding players through one simulated, complete gameplay loop before teleporting them to the main server. Additionally, an in-game, "notebook-styled" guide will allow on-demand rule access without breaking immersion.

## 7. Industry Context: Solving the Tower Obby Replayability Crisis

The "Tower Obby" niche on Roblox suffers from a fundamental design flaw: lack of long-term replayability. The dominant design pattern dictates that once players complete the static obstacle course, they receive "god powers" (flying carpets, instant-kill swords), inevitably leading to trolling, restarting from zero, or leaving entirely.

[*Tower of Hell*](https://www.roblox.com/games/1962086868/Tower-of-Hell), a highly successful outlier, attempts to solve this through extreme difficulty (instant death, no checkpoints) and randomized preset levels. However, this approach relies on gatekeeping, exploiting players' desire to conquer near-impossible challenges, resulting in a vast majority never actually winning.

*Tower of Literal Randomness* solves this through pure procedural generation. Because bricks spawn and despawn dynamically, no "cheat sheet" or memorizable pattern exists. We deliberately lowered baseline difficulty to "difficult but attainable" to prevent the extreme spectrum where players quit due to perceived 0.1% win rates. Furthermore, the "Modifier" system (Easy: lenient damage, climb all bricks; Hard: forced first-person, foggy visibility) accommodates different skill levels by twisting *player properties* rather than altering core mechanics for everyone. This ensures the game remains a fair, infinitely replayable test of adaptability rather than static memorization, addressing Chapter 2's emphasis on replayability as a key industry consideration (Kwong, Chapter 2).

## 8. Conclusion

*Tower of Literal Randomness* demonstrates how procedural generation, thoughtful world-building synthesis, and iterative playtesting can revitalize a saturated genre. By addressing the fundamental replayability crisis through dynamic mechanics rather than extreme difficulty gatekeeping, we created an accessible yet challenging experience that respects player time while maintaining competitive integrity. The project successfully applied subject lexicon from Chapters 1-9, particularly the synthesis of rules, mechanics, and narrative (Chapter 6), the strategic use of materiality and abstraction (Chapters 7-8), and the balance of creativity with technical constraints (Chapter 9). Future development will focus on onboarding optimization and LOD implementation to maintain performance while enhancing visual fidelity.

## Appendix A: Official Game Rules (v0.8.18)
*Note: As per subject guidelines, the rules document is exempt from the 2000-word limit.*

### A.1 Match Structure & Timing
- **Intermission Duration:** 30 seconds
- **Voting Phase:** 15 seconds  
- **Round Initialization:** 5 seconds
- **Active Round Duration:** 5 minutes (300 seconds)
- **Return Mechanic:** Players press a return button to immediately initiate another climb from the ground upon reaching the summit.

### A.2 Core Mechanics & Physics
**Spawn Logic & Size:**
- Bricks spawn completely randomly within the 150x150x1000-stud arena. 
- Sizes are determined by a random preset algorithm (e.g., ranging from 8x8x8 to 20x20x20 studs).

**Brick Types:**
- **Red (Harmful):** Deals HP damage (Max HP: 100 default). At 0 HP, the player ragdolls, dies, and respawns at the bottom. A visual copy of the player's dead body is left behind.
- **White (Neutral):** Standard platform, no special properties.
- **Blue (Climbable):** Primary progression surface.
- **Black (Trampoline):** Deliberately flat (Y-axis capped to 1 stud), bounces player upward.

### A.3 Progression, Economy & Skills
**XP Economy:**
- Players do not earn XP during the climb. XP is awarded in a lump sum only when the 5-minute round concludes, or if the player physically disconnects from the game.
- By default, climbing 1 stud is worth 1 XP in the final tally. This can be manipulated by Modifiers.

**Energy & Skill System:**
- Skills do not use time cooldowns. Instead, they consume Energy (1 to 3 units per use).
- Energy is generated by physically interacting with tower bricks (1 Energy per brick interacted).
- **Loadout Rules:** Players can equip only 1 skill at a time. The equipped skill persists until manually deselected. Skills are permanently owned once purchased in the Shop. 
- **State Changes:** Skills, modifiers, or any local game-changing states can *only* be applied while the player is standing on the ground.

### A.4 Modifiers (Difficulty System)
Modifiers are classified into a list of "easy items" (which decreases difficulty), and "hard items" (which increases difficulty). Players are free to toggle whichever they desire. Enabling modifier items may affect XP earned.

### A.5 Gamemodes
Gamemodes include but are not limited to:
- **Classic:** Default spawn algorithm.
- **Legacy:** Only white bricks spawn. This is the absolute vanilla version.
- **Y4K:** Spawn algorithm altered; every brick is extremely vertically long (4x the standard height).
- **Overheat:** Red bricks spawn at double their standard size.
