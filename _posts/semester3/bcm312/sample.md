# The Chicken Who Eats Itself: Semiotic Erasure and the Expiration of Comical Suffering in Samyang Buldak

## 1. Introduction

### 1.1 Contextualizing the "Spicy Challenge"

In the global fast-moving consumer goods (FMCG) aisle, Samyang Buldak's flagship mascot, Hochi, presents a striking visual paradox: a cartoon chicken sweating in exaggerated agony, seemingly enduring the very spice it is selling. Prevailing academic assumption in consumer psychology explains that when faced with the "meat paradox" (cognitive dissonance of caring for animals while eating them), the food industry employs "moral disengagement" to alleviate consumer guilt (Bastian & Loughnan, 2017). However, a close examination of Buldak's packaging reveals that this assumption fundamentally misreads the brand's visual rhetoric. Its flagship mascot, Hochi's agonized, absurd design offers no ethical comfort, which signals no intention to offer moral absolution or make the audience "feel good." Instead, Hochi functions as an instrument of total semiotic erasure. By forcing the chicken to perform meme-driven comical suffering, the packaging completely overwrites the biological imagination of the animal, replacing it with a participatory spectacle of spice endurance.

This distinction between moral relief and semiotic erasure is the core focus of this research. Rather than asking how the mascot resolves ethical tension, this study asks how it renders the tension entirely irrelevant. To answer this, the study investigates how Hochi's "comical suffering" frames chicken-flavored consumption as humorous, and how consumers de facto decode this rhetoric when confronted with the reality of the product's artificial ingredients.

---

### 1.2 The Corporate Pivot

The timeliness of this inquiry is amplified by a critical shift in the brand's corporate strategy. In June 2026, Samyang Foods announced the replacement of Hochi with a new, in-house mascot, PEPPO, citing a strategic shift toward internal intellectual property ownership and cost reduction (Chosun Ilbo, 2026). This transition transforms the study from a static packaging analysis into a dynamic case study of a visual rhetoric lifecycle. It raises a pragmatic question: when a brand abandons "comical suffering" for a sanitized, generic IP asset, what specific brand equity is actually lost?

---

### 1.3 Research Question and Methodological Overview

To triangulate these dynamics, this research employs a multi-method approach. A comparative visual analysis of core Buldak variants (Classic, 2x Spicy, Carbonara) establishes the baseline of the "suffering" aesthetic. This is cross-referenced with an exploratory consumer survey (n=32) that measures visual hierarchy, ingredient awareness, and the physical consumption experience, supplemented by an autoethnographic reflection on the sensory reality of the "spice challenge." Ultimately, this paper argues that Hochi's success lies not in hiding the biological animal behind a veil of ethical comfort, but in deleting the biological animal from the consumer's mental map entirely—a visual strategy that has now reached its corporate expiration date.

---

## 2. The Visual Rhetoric of Comical Suffering

A comparative visual analysis of the core Samyang Buldak variants reveals that Hochi's "comical suffering" is not a universal brand trait, but a highly calibrated visual rhetoric strategy deployed specifically to gatekeep the "spicy challenge" experience.

![Comparison between spicy and casual variants](/assets/images/bcm312_assets/final-blog/spicy-casual-hochi.png)

As the visual matrix demonstrates, there is a clear split between the spicy line and the non-spicy line. The spicy variants exhibit a torturous experience, serving as a visual gatekeeper. The non-spicy variants signal general appeal and leisure.

This split addresses how Hochi employs comical suffering to frame consumption. The primary finding is that **the "suffering" in question is primarily not about the animal, but rather the consumer's.** By staging Hochi's agony exclusively in the "spicy challenge" variants, **the packaging aestheticizes the pain** of eating extreme spice, transforming it from a potential deterrent into a comedic, participatory meme. The consumer is **not meant to feel sympathy** for the chicken (feeling for him), **but rather to engage in physical empathy** (feeling with him, or Einfuhlung) as they attempt to survive their own meal.

Meanwhile, the "content" Hochi of the Carbonara line signals that the product is approachable, enjoyable, and free of the "torturous" gatekeeping of the spice challenge. The visual rhetoric is not resolving ethical tension about eating animals; it is curating two distinct consumption narratives: one of shared endurance (spicy line) and one of general appeal (non-spicy line). In both cases, the biological animal is rendered entirely invisible.

## 3. Consumer Decoding and the "Invisible Animal"

While the visual analysis establishes how Hochi's design operates as an expectation anchor, the exploratory survey (n=32) reveals how consumers de facto decode this rhetoric. The data confirms that Hochi does not resolve ethical tension regarding meat consumption; he renders the biological animal entirely irrelevant.

### 3.1 Reframing the "Sight of Suffering"

To test whether the packaging neutralizes the "sight of suffering," respondents were shown the isolated, plain design of Hochi, followed by the full "2x Spicy" packaging context.

![Q5a](/assets/images/bcm312_assets/final-blog/Q5a.png)

When viewing the isolated character, reactions leaned toward absurdity (Q5a: 37.5% "Funny/Hilarious," 28.1% "Confusing/Weird"). Only 3.1% found it "Distressing." 

![Q6](/assets/images/bcm312_assets/final-blog/Q6.png)
![Q7](/assets/images/bcm312_assets/final-blog/Q7.png)

The survey further reveals a critical tension in Hochi's design: while respondents rated his aesthetic appeal low (average Q6 score: 2.1/5), they simultaneously rated his attention-grabbing effectiveness remarkably high (average Q7 score: 4.2/5). This 'ugly but potent' dynamic confirms that the brand prioritizes disruptive visual rhetoric over traditional aesthetic appeal. Hochi's weirdness is not a design flaw, but a deliberate mechanism that makes him impossible to ignore, creating the comedic hook that the packaging then reframes as a spice warning.

![Q9](/assets/images/bcm312_assets/final-blog/Q9.png)

When placed in the full packaging context, the visual hierarchy shifted dramatically. The majority decoded the mascot as a comedic spice warning (Q9: "It's funny / I get the joke or meme" or "It looks way too spicy"). 

![Q10: Ethical discomfort perception](/assets/images/bcm312_assets/final-blog/Q10.png)

Crucially, 100% of respondents dismissed any notion of "animal distress or ethical discomfort." The packaging successfully reframes exaggerated agony from an ethical provocation into a sensory warning.

---

### 3.2 The Myth of Ethical Friction and Total Semiotic Erasure

The survey data shatters the academic assumption that anthropomorphic mascots function as "moral disengagement" tools to alleviate the "meat paradox" (Bastian & Loughnan, 2017; Piazza et al., 2015). When asked if they were aware that Buldak contains no actual chicken meat, 93.8% of respondents indicated they either "knew for sure" (46.9%) or "suspected it didn't" (46.9%).

![Q15a](/assets/images/bcm312_assets/final-blog/Q15a.png)
![Q15b](/assets/images/bcm312_assets/final-blog/Q15b.png)

Furthermore, when confronted with this fact, nearly 70% of respondents expressed total apathy. The most selected option was "It doesn't change anything; I expect instant noodles to use artificial flavors" (46.9%), followed by "I don't really care either way; the mascot is just a logo to me" (21.9%).

This indicates that the "ethical friction" academics assume exists is a mirage in hyper-processed FMCG. Consumers do not apply the "meat paradox" to artificial flavor powders. As Una Chaudhuri (2003) asserts, through anthropomorphism, "animals are forced to perform us - our fantasies and fears." Hochi is the ultimate manifestation of this erasure. By forcing the chicken to perform absurd, meme-driven human comedy, the packaging completely severs the mascot from biological livestock. Hochi is not read as a farm animal; he is read as a brand IP, a "jester archetype," or simply a logo. He does not make the consumer feel good about the meat; he deletes the biological animal from the consumer's mental map entirely.

## 4. The Autoethnographic Reality: Shared Endurance

The visual analysis and survey data establish how Hochi's rhetoric operates at the level of perception. However, the "sight of suffering" is not merely visual; it is bodily. To triangulate the empirical findings with the phenomenological reality of consumption, the following autoethnographic reflection documents a physical encounter with Buldak Original.

### 4.1 The Sensory Trap

The author approached the Original variant with a specific constraint: a personal food aversion to cheese, eliminating the non-spicy variants. This left the author "forced into" the spicy line due to academic obligation, having effectively "picked the wrong option."

Upon opening the packaging, the first encounter was not with spice, but with confusion. There is only a singular sauce packet. This creates an inescapable binary: either go all in and "get flamed," or add a little and taste bland noodles. The sauce packet itself is deceptively engineered; it smells rather sweet and enticing. Intuitively, if you cannot smell spiciness, you go all in. But this is a trap, because this is "essentially how you end up looking like Hochi later on." The sweet olfactory cue functions as a siren song, luring the consumer into the full dose before the delayed capsaicin response registers.

---

### 4.2 The Hazing Ritual

In the Buldak consumption ritual, the consumer's body is forced into a physical *Einfuhlung*: the burning mouth, sweating forehead, and tearing eyes directly mirror the mascot's "flipped blank" expression. The consumer becomes Hochi.

However, this "shared endurance" is not a joyful, communal meme. It operates closer to a hazing ritual enforced by peer surveillance. When the author expressed physical distress, peers dismissed the reaction, calling the author "weak." The survey data corroborates this: multiple respondents explicitly selected "I see other people eat it, I eat it (monkey see monkey do)" as their primary motivation. Furthermore, approximately 20% of respondents rated themselves as "1 - I actively dislike it," yet many of these same respondents rated their physical experience as a 4 or 5 on the "Experience Match" scale. They hate the noodles, but confirm their suffering perfectly matches Hochi's visual agony. The packaging did not lie; it warned them of a torture they were socially pressured into enduring.

---

### 4.3 Monetizing the Repulsion

The most pragmatic revelation is the aftermath. Post-consumption, the author felt "fear and repulsed rather than pulled and attracted," hesitant to eat the remaining servings. In the Hong Kong supermarket context, Buldak is sold exclusively in bundles of five packets. The brand's strategy is cunning: the visual rhetoric of Hochi's comical suffering successfully sells the meme and gets the consumer to buy the ticket, but "they earn 4 more packets from a customer who would not look back." The mascot creates the cultural pull; the bundle packaging monetizes the regret.

---

## 5. The Mascot Lifecycle: Brand Equity vs. Corporate IP Strategy

The visual rhetoric of "comical suffering" has been the cornerstone of Buldak's global identity since 2014. However, Samyang's June 2026 announcement to replace Hochi with PEPPO to cut IP costs (Chosun Ilbo, 2026) provides a unique opportunity to evaluate the specific brand equity at risk when this character is retired.

PEPPO, a stylized bird with a cleaner, more modern design, represents a "safer" corporate asset designed for global scalability. He is less "weird," less "suffering," and more conventionally cute. However, the survey data reveals this corporate sanitization carries significant risk. When asked which mascot is a better fit for a "spicy instant noodles" brand, the results were nuanced:

![Q17a: Mascot preference comparison](/assets/images/bcm312_assets/final-blog/Q17a.png)

Combined, nearly 80% of respondents either explicitly preferred Hochi or believed the two should co-exist. Qualitative responses provide sharp insight into why consumers reject the pivot. Those who preferred Hochi emphasized his distinctiveness: "Hochi is iconic... identifiable from a kilometre away. The new look just erases all identity," and "Hochi grabs attention by being slightly unsettling and weird... Peppo is cute but it does not stand out... it feels like it tries to conform." One respondent bluntly noted, "peppo looks soulless as fuck."

Consumers value Hochi precisely because he is not a "bog-standard" mascot. His chaotic, meme-able energy resonates with the demographics that drive Buldak's viral success. PEPPO is read as "safe" and "conforming"—traits attractive to corporate IP lawyers but repellant to the brand's core consumer base.

The transition from Hochi to PEPPO represents a shift from cultural meme to corporate asset. When consumers eat Buldak and sweat, they are physically mirroring Hochi's expression; they are *becoming* Hochi. PEPPO, with his cleaner lines and lack of "suffering," breaks this participatory loop. He is an observer, not a participant. By replacing Hochi, Samyang risks trading a chaotic, high-equity "jester" archetype for a safe, low-equity "mascot" archetype. The corporate pivot may optimize the company's IP ledger, but it bankrupts its cultural capital. The message was always the burn. The chicken was just the guide.

---

## 6. Conclusion

### 6.1 Synthesizing the Findings

This research set out to investigate how Samyang Buldak's mascot, Hochi, employs "comical suffering" to frame chicken-flavored consumption as humorous rather than ethically troubling. Through multi-method triangulation, the study reveals a complex mechanism of semiotic erasure and participatory endurance that challenges prevailing academic assumptions.

The visual analysis and survey data confirmed that Hochi's exaggerated agony is aestheticized and reframed by the packaging into a comedic spice warning. Crucially, Hochi does not function as a "moral shield" to alleviate consumer guilt. Instead, the survey revealed widespread apathy toward the ingredient reality, indicating that the "ethical friction" academics assume exists is largely a mirage in hyper-processed FMCG. Hochi operates in a space where the biological animal has already been rendered invisible through total semiotic erasure.

The autoethnographic reflection further triangulated these findings, confirming that the "shared endurance" is a phenomenological reality. The consumer's body is forced to enact the mascot's suffering, transforming the "sight of suffering" into a participatory hazing ritual.

---

### 6.2 Answering the Research Question

How does Hochi employ comical suffering to frame consumption as humorous? The answer is that he **enacts** the very action himself. Rather than witnessing the consumer as a passive symbol, Hochi is a fictional active participant in the "spicy challenge" meme. This strategy is highly effective because it bypasses ethical friction entirely. Consumers do not feel guilty about eating "chicken" noodles because Hochi has successfully erased the biological chicken from their mental map, replacing it with a cartoon jester who is on board with the joke.

---

### 6.3 Final Thoughts: The Expiration Date of Comical Suffering

Ultimately, the announced transition from Hochi to PEPPO marks the potential expiration date of this visual rhetoric strategy. By sanitizing the mascot, Samyang risks severing the brand from the very "spicy challenge" identity that made it a global phenomenon. This study demonstrates that in global FMCG, anthropomorphic humor serves as effective behavioral mirroring. Hochi's comical suffering does not hide the animal; it deletes it. And in doing so, it turns the consumer's own body into the final canvas for the brand's visual rhetoric.
