# Bear Witness: Contextual Essay

Today, the act of purchasing commodities is only a finger’s distance away. In pursuit of revenue gains, markets increasingly engineer artificial scarcity and use the psychological manipulation of “fear of missing out” (FOMO). This is especially visible in collectible and fandom-based markets, where products function less as objects and more as culturally desirable experiences (IGN, 2024; Wang & Hancock, 2024). In this context, our group developed Bear Witness, a digital artefact designed to encourage young adults to reflect on overconsumption without feeling lectured or shamed (The Standard, 2026).

---

# PROJECT DEVELOPMENT TRAJECTORY

## Project Concept and Design Decisions

Bear Witness is a visual novel created in Ren’Py that uses interactive storytelling and a gacha-inspired mechanic to explore the waste and emotional manipulation rooted in consumer culture. Rather than presenting overconsumption as an individual moral failure, the project frames it as a broader media and social problem shaped by platform culture in marketing tactics and emotional appeal (Lins et al., 2021). The artefact was developed through an iterative process shaped by peer review, beta feedback, and repeated refinement. This contextual essay traces that development trajectory, identifies key learning moments while reflecting on how the project evolved in response to audience feedback and production constraints.

Our target audience was young adults aged 18–25, a demographic that often rejects top-down, preachy messaging about their consumption habits (Francis and Hoefel, 2018). This shaped two important design choices early in development. First, we decided that the deliverable needed to be hands-on rather than lecture-like. A visual novel format allowed us to create a narrative experience that could engage the player through, say, choices, pacing, or even the atmosphere while still remaining manageable within the constraints of the subject. Second, we had to manage the player’s psychological identification. If we cast the player as a consumer or corporate boss, they would immediately trigger defensiveness, prompting them to dismiss the character as "flawed" and avoid self-reflection. To bypass this, the player assumes the role of a discarded teddy bear. This removes implicit blame from the player, forcing them to observe the cycle of consumer waste from the perspective of a commodity (McKinsey & Company, 2024).

This framing also helped us communicate the emotional consequences of overconsumption more effectively. The visual novel also specifically deployed **memetic framing within the dialogue** to validate the satire through shared cultural knowledge (Hart, 2023). For example, the narrative references the viral "$600 Furina incident", which is a well-known gacha community meme about an engagement being called off after a partner was caught stealing credit card funds for upgrading a game character, Furina. By embedding community-specific jokes, we utilize the audience’s own casual knowledge to critique a social problem without ever sounding like a lecture.

## Peer Review I: First Feedback Loop

Following our Week 4 project pitch, we engaged in our first feedback loop with our peers. The response to the core concept was highly positive. It is noted that reviewers consistently praised the originality of the "unwanted teddy bear" perspective, believing that the darkly humorous tone made the heavy subject of overconsumption digestible and engaging for the target age group. This was important because the project avoided directly accusing the player and instead encouraged reflection through irony and perspective. 

However, the peer reviews also revealed a critical methodological paradox:

As we introduced a "gacha simulation" mechanic, the story risked contradicting the project’s anti-overconsumption message. This was a valuable insight because it revealed a contradiction in the project’s structure. The same mechanic that was meant to expose compulsive consumer behaviour could also end up making that behaviour feel enjoyable. Peers pointed out that the project needed a clearer and more documented feedback strategy, especially if we wanted to show that the final artefact was shaped by real user responses rather than only by internal group decisions.

This feedback became one of the most important learning moments in the project. It pushed us to think carefully about how meaning is produced through interaction. A critique depends not just on what a story says but also on how the audience experiences the system. That understanding guided our next round of revisions.

## Beta Development: Tightening Scope and Message

In response to the first round of feedback, and guided by the OODA Loop (Observe, Orient, Decide, Act) (Boyd, 1987) and the FIST (Fast, Inexpensive, Simple, Tiny) design principles (Ward, 2015), we executed two major iterations for our Project Beta.

The first was to tighten the narrative scope. Originally, we had planned for the prologue to branch into multiple narrative routes quite early. While this sounded ambitious, it would have made the build more difficult to test and much harder to complete within the available time. We therefore replaced the early branching system with a lighter attribute-based structure. Players still make meaningful choices at the beginning of the game (e.g., scrambling the bin, calling for help), but those choices now contribute to the protagonist’s character traits rather than splitting the story into multiple large branches straight away.

This decision was important because it aligned the project more closely with the FIST design principle: fast, inexpensive, simple, and tiny. Instead of building a large and unstable system, we focused on something manageable that still preserved player agency. This also made the game easier to refine in later stages because we could focus on the quality of individual scenes rather than spreading our attention across too many narrative paths. From a development perspective, this was a practical lesson in scope management: sometimes a smaller system produces a stronger result than an overextended one.

We then directly addressed the "gacha paradox" identified in Peer Review I. To curb the risk where the gacha scene is interpreted as encouraging, we added the context of an arguing couple into the gacha scene to reframe the expectation of potential wins into harm prevention, resulting in a high stake and critical atmosphere. 

## Peer Review II: Refinement and Polishing

During the Week 9 Peer Review II, these iterations were validated as reviewers explicitly praised the scope tightening as a "smart, pragmatic call" that maintained narrative cohesion without overcomplicating development. This mattered because the project had become easier to follow and less likely to lose focus. The revised gacha sequence was also seen as more effective because its tone now better matched the project’s critical intention.

However, reviewers also noted that the static nature of the placeholder art in the Beta made it difficult to emotionally resonate with the characters. In response, our final iterations focused on visual and micro-interaction polish. We unified the sizing of all character art sprites to ensure long-term visual consistency. To bridge the emotional gap, we created multiple expressive sprite variations for key scenarios, allowing the characters' reactions to dynamically reflect the player’s attribute choices. Certain scenes were also tweaked to involve subtle micro-interactions, such as the gacha machine violently shaking upon a failed pull, to enhance the tactile, stressful feedback loop we aimed to create. These changes were subtle, but they strengthened the overall atmosphere and helped the project communicate its message more effectively.

# KEY LEARNING MOMENTS

## Character Design: Visual Assets

A major part of the project involved developing the visual assets, particularly the character sprites and logo. For visual novels, the art is a very important feature as it is the game’s selling point. In total, our game has seven different characters. Our group first established basic outlines for each character, including clothing, colour schemes, and accessories, so that the designs would be consistent with their roles in the story. We also agreed that the characters should also have multiple different expressions to enhance the visual aspects of our game, and we gave them a set of different expressions that were tailored to fit their personalities. It would also keep it engaging for the player and gives the game a more captivating environment due to the expressions matching the characters dialogue. 

Character designing was the most challenging aspect of creating the assets as it was quite time consuming and required adjustments if something didn’t seem quite right. Because visual novel assets need to be readable at a glance, we had to avoid overcomplicating the designs. At the same time, the characters still needed enough detail to feel distinct. The easiest character to draw was the bear as it is not humanoid, and the design was not complex to execute. This experience taught us that good asset design goes beyond aesthetics and also requires clarity and production efficiency (Norman, 2013).

The logo was also designed with the project’s themes in mind. We used the bear’s head as the central image because it functions as both the protagonist and the mascot of the game. We knew having it as our logo would make our game instantly recognisable due to the unique design. The closed eyes were an intentional symbol of irony, linking directly to the title Bear Witness. This gave the logo a conceptual dimension rather than making it merely decorative.

## Pitch & Beta: Frontend Presentation

From the perspective of presentation, one of the main roles was editing the pitch and beta videos. This required balancing clarity and visual consistency. A key part of the workflow was managing dependencies. The voice over had to be completed before timing could be finalised, but we used the waiting period to gather footage, screenshots, and supporting visuals so that editing could move forward efficiently once the audio was ready. This approach matched the project’s FIST mindset, keeping the process fast and simple while being realistic within our time constraints (Ward, 2015).

The videos were edited in Adobe After Effects because it allowed efficiency for the specific kind of polish our videos needed (clean fades, smooth panning/zooming, controlled jitter for emphasis) without making the presentation feel overly busy. Since the topic was overconsumption, it was important that the video remained clear and readable rather than becoming visually overwhelming. Section labels, lower-thirds, and brief on-screen callouts were used to guide viewers through the structure of the presentation. These elements helped the audience understand what they were looking at without turning the video into a lecture. 

Another important lesson was that design choices should support the message rather than compete with it. For that reason, background music was avoided in some sections because it risked distracting from the voice-over and making the presentation feel too heavily produced. The voice itself carried the tone effectively, so keeping the audio clean made the message easier to follow. This experience showed that restraint can sometimes be more effective than embellishment when the goal is clarity and persuasion.

## Curation of Engaging Voice-Over

The voice-over for the visual novel also required careful preparation and recording. Clear audio was essential, so the recording process began by making sure there was minimal background noise and that the script was ready to be delivered smoothly. Each paragraph of the script was read through before recording to reduce the chance of mispronunciation and to improve overall delivery. The audio was then recorded using the Voice Memos app, replayed for quality checking, and sent to the group chat as an MP3 file once it was confirmed to be usable. This process showed the importance of reviewing each recording before sharing it, since a polished final product depends on both technical clarity and careful preparation.

# Conclusion: The Takeaway

Overall, Bear Witness grew from a broad idea about overconsumption into a more focused and coherent digital artefact through repeated feedback and revision. The most important lesson we took from the process was that critique is most effective when the form of the artefact reinforces the message it wants to communicate. Our early version had a strong concept, but later revisions made the project more effective by narrowing the scope, clarifying the interactive mechanics, and improving the visual presentation.

The development process also showed us that making a socially useful digital artefact takes more than having a strong opinion. It requires sensitivity to the audience, careful attention to tone, and a willingness to adapt in response to criticism. By using feedback constructively, we were able to refine the game into a project that not only represents our ideas about overconsumption but also demonstrates how digital media can make that issue visible in an engaging and reflective way. In that sense, Bear Witness is both a creative product and a record of what we learned through making it.
